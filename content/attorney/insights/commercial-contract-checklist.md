A Practical Guide for Small Businesses

A commercial contract can look straightforward at first glance. The price is agreed upon, the services are described, and both parties are ready to move forward.

But the most important provisions are not always the most obvious ones.

Before signing your next business agreement, consider these five questions.

## 1. Are the Parties' Obligations Clearly Defined?

A good contract should explain exactly what each party is expected to do.

Check the scope of work, deliverables, deadlines, payment responsibilities, and any conditions that must be satisfied.

Vague language can create disagreements about what was actually promised.

Practical tip: If two reasonable people could interpret an important obligation differently, consider clarifying it before signing.

## 2. What Happens If Something Goes Wrong?

Business relationships do not always proceed as planned.

Review the provisions addressing breach of contract, remedies, warranties, indemnification, and limitations of liability.

Pay particular attention to whether the agreement places disproportionate financial risk on your business.

Practical tip: Don't focus only on what happens when the relationship succeeds. Consider the consequences if the other party fails to perform.

## 3. Can You Terminate the Agreement?

Some contracts are easy to enter but difficult or expensive to exit.

Look for automatic renewals, minimum commitments, early termination fees, notice requirements, and post-termination obligations.

An agreement that seems reasonable today may become burdensome as your business changes.

Practical tip: Understand both how the agreement begins and how it ends.

## 4. Who Owns the Work, Data, and Intellectual Property?

For technology, manufacturing, consulting, and service agreements, ownership provisions can be particularly important.

Determine who owns existing intellectual property, newly developed materials, customer data, confidential information, and work product.

Also review the rights each party retains after the agreement terminates.

Practical tip: Payment for work does not necessarily mean ownership of every related intellectual property right.

## 5. Which Law Governs the Agreement, and Where Will Disputes Be Resolved?

A contract may involve businesses located in different states or countries.

The governing-law clause and dispute-resolution provisions can significantly affect the cost, procedure, and available remedies if a dispute arises.

Check whether disputes must be resolved in court or arbitration and whether the designated location is practical for your business.

Practical tip: Do not assume that your local law will govern simply because your company is located there.

## A Final Thought

The purpose of reviewing a commercial contract is not to eliminate every conceivable risk. It is to understand the obligations, identify material risks, and make informed business decisions before signing.

A careful review today may help avoid expensive misunderstandings tomorrow.
